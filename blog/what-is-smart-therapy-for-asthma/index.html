What Is SMART Therapy, and Can One Inhaler Be Both Your Daily and Rescue Inhaler?

The short answer: SMART means single maintenance and reliever therapy. You use one inhaler that holds a steroid (budesonide or beclometasone) plus formoterol, both every day and for quick relief. It works because formoterol opens the airways fast, so every rescue puff also delivers a dose of steroid. Both the 2026 GINA Summary Guide and the 2020 NHLBI focused update call it a preferred approach, but the US Symbicort label still says the inhaler is not for acute symptoms.

What exactly is SMART, and what is it called elsewhere?

In the classic setup you carry two inhalers. A steroid or steroid-LABA inhaler controls inflammation each day. A separate albuterol inhaler handles flare-ups. If you want the basics of that split, see our guide to rescue versus controller inhalers.

SMART folds both jobs into one device. You take a set number of puffs morning and evening, then take extra puffs of the same inhaler when symptoms flare. US guidelines call this SMART. GINA, the Global Initiative for Asthma, calls it MART (maintenance-and-reliever therapy). The terms mean the same thing.

GINA also describes a lighter version for mild asthma called AIR-only (anti-inflammatory reliever). There is no daily dose. You use low-dose ICS-formoterol only when you have symptoms.

Why does it have to be formoterol?

Most long-acting bronchodilators are slow to kick in, so they cannot serve as a reliever. Formoterol is different. The Symbicort label describes formoterol as a long-acting beta2-agonist "with a rapid onset of action." In the label's pivotal studies, the median time to clinically significant bronchodilation was within 15 minutes.

That speed lets one drug pair do two jobs. Each time you feel tight and reach for relief, you also get more inhaled steroid at the moment inflammation is rising. GINA states the rule plainly: "ICS-LABA inhalers that do not include formoterol cannot be used as MART." The NHLBI guide adds a specific warning. Do not use ICS-formoterol as a reliever if your daily controller is ICS-salmeterol (such as fluticasone-salmeterol).

What do GINA and US guidelines recommend?

The two major guidelines agree on the core idea but differ on who qualifies.

  • GINA 2026. GINA splits adult and teen treatment into two tracks. In Track 1, the preferred track, the reliever at every step is low-dose ICS-formoterol. At Steps 1 and 2 it is used as needed only. At Steps 3 to 5 the same inhaler is also the daily maintenance dose (MART). GINA says it prefers Track 1 because "there is strong evidence that it reduces the risk of severe exacerbations" compared with a SABA-based Track 2, with similar symptom control.
  • NHLBI 2020 focused update. For people ages 4 and older with moderate to severe persistent asthma, the Expert Panel recommends ICS-formoterol in one inhaler as daily controller and reliever. It says SMART fits Step 3 (low-dose ICS) and Step 4 (medium-dose ICS). It names people who had a severe flare in the past year as "particularly good candidates."

Note the difference. The 2020 US update did not endorse as-needed ICS-formoterol alone for mild asthma. For ages 12 and older with mild persistent asthma, it listed daily low-dose ICS or as-needed ICS taken with SABA. GINA goes further and uses ICS-formoterol as the reliever from Step 1.

The NHLBI guide sets a maximum number of puffs per day, with a lower limit for children ages 4 to 11 than for older patients. Because US labels do not cover SMART, the exact puff counts come from your clinician, not the package insert. Your own plan comes from your clinician, and it should be written into your asthma action plan.

Does SMART actually prevent more asthma attacks?

The main payoff is fewer severe flare-ups, not better day-to-day symptoms. Two large reviews show this.

  • A 2018 meta-analysis in JAMA pooled 16 randomized trials with 22,748 patients. In people 12 and older, SMART cut the risk of exacerbations compared with the same dose of ICS-LABA plus a SABA reliever (risk ratio 0.68). That was an absolute drop of 6.4 percentage points. It also beat a higher dose of ICS-LABA (risk ratio 0.77). Data in children 4 to 11 also favored SMART, but the authors called that evidence limited (341 children).
  • A 2013 Cochrane review of 13 trials in adults found fewer flare-ups needing oral steroids with a single inhaler. Against higher-dose budesonide, 18 of every 100 adults needed a steroid course over 11 months, versus 11 with the single inhaler. The review found weak evidence for fewer hospital stays. Every trial was sponsored by the inhaler's maker, and most were open-label.

The NHLBI panel reached a similar view. It wrote that SMART "consistently reduced asthma exacerbations requiring unscheduled medical visits or systemic corticosteroids." It found no difference in documented harms compared with standard regimens.

Is SMART approved in the US, and which inhalers are used?

This is where guidelines and labels part ways. GINA's Track 1 example uses budesonide-formoterol 160/4.5 mcg or beclometasone-formoterol 100/6 mcg. In the US, the budesonide-formoterol option is Symbicort and its generics. Beclometasone-formoterol is sold in other countries.

The current Symbicort label on DailyMed covers asthma treatment in people 6 and older, dosed as 2 inhalations twice daily. It also says: "SYMBICORT is NOT indicated for the relief of acute bronchospasm." The patient counseling section tells prescribers to provide a short-acting reliever such as albuterol. So when a US doctor prescribes SMART, it is an off-label use backed by the guidelines, not by the label.

Two practical points follow. GINA tells clinicians to "check local regulatory and payer criteria," because insurance may limit refills. NHLBI warns that a 1-month supply sized for maintenance "may not last a month" once you also use it for relief.

How is Airsupra different from SMART?

Airsupra combines albuterol with budesonide. It is also an anti-inflammatory reliever, but it is not a SMART inhaler. The Airsupra label says it is for "as-needed treatment or prevention of bronchoconstriction and to reduce the risk of exacerbations" in adults 18 and older. The label lists no daily maintenance dose. Its labeled limit is no more than 6 doses (12 inhalations) in 24 hours.

Because albuterol is short-acting, it cannot be a maintenance drug. GINA places ICS-SABA relievers like this in Track 2, the alternative track. So Airsupra replaces your rescue inhaler, while your separate daily controller stays in place. SMART replaces both.

Who may not be a good fit for SMART?

SMART is not the right fit for everyone. Points from the guidelines worth raising with your clinician:

  • You use a non-formoterol combination inhaler and prefer not to switch.
  • Cost or formulary rules make frequent refills hard, as NHLBI notes.
  • You often hit the daily maximum. That is a sign to call your doctor, not to keep puffing.

Like any steroid inhaler, SMART can cause thrush or hoarseness. Rinsing after use helps; see our post on steroid inhalers and oral thrush. If you are not sure whether new chest symptoms are asthma at all, symptom.md covers how to sort out common symptoms before a visit.

The bottom line

Yes, one inhaler can be both your daily and rescue inhaler, but only if it combines a steroid with formoterol. GINA 2026 makes ICS-formoterol its preferred reliever, and the 2020 NHLBI update recommends SMART for moderate to severe persistent asthma from age 4. Trials show fewer severe flare-ups than with a separate albuterol reliever. The US Symbicort label still lists it as not for acute symptoms, so this is a guideline-based choice you make with your clinician. Airsupra is a different tool: an as-needed reliever only.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use my Symbicort as a rescue inhaler?

Only if your clinician has put you on a SMART plan. The US Symbicort label says it is not indicated for relief of acute bronchospasm, but GINA and NHLBI guidelines support ICS-formoterol as a reliever. Follow the written plan you get from your doctor.

Can I do SMART with Advair or Breo?

No. Advair uses salmeterol and Breo uses vilanterol, which do not act fast enough to be relievers. GINA says inhalers without formoterol cannot be used as MART, and NHLBI warns against using ICS-formoterol as a reliever on top of ICS-salmeterol.

Do I still need to carry albuterol on SMART?

On SMART, the ICS-formoterol inhaler is your reliever, so most plans do not include routine albuterol. Some clinicians still prescribe a backup. Ask your doctor what your plan says to do in a severe attack.

Is SMART safe for children?

The 2020 NHLBI update recommends SMART from age 4 for moderate to severe persistent asthma, with a lower daily puff limit for ages 4 to 11. The JAMA meta-analysis found fewer flare-ups in children, but the evidence came from only 341 kids. The Symbicort label covers asthma treatment from age 6.

Why does my inhaler run out early on SMART?

Your reliever puffs come from the same canister as your daily puffs. NHLBI notes that a 1-month supply sized for maintenance may not last a month. Tell your clinician so the prescription can cover both uses.
