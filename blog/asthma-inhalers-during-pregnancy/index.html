Is It Safe To Use An Inhaler During Pregnancy?

September 11, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes, for the inhalers used to treat asthma. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) says inhaled corticosteroids should be a mainstay of treatment for every pregnant patient with asthma. Poorly controlled asthma carries clearer risks to mother and baby than the inhalers that control it. The more common error in pregnancy is stopping an inhaler, not using one.

Why do doctors treat uncontrolled asthma as the bigger risk?

Asthma is the most common chronic lung condition in pregnancy. ACOG's Clinical Practice Guideline on asthma in pregnancy (Obstetrics & Gynecology, July 2025) states that asthma affects up to 10% of pregnancies and confers risk to both mother and child.

The guideline lists the maternal outcomes linked to asthma as preeclampsia, preterm labor, and a higher rate of cesarean delivery. On the infant side it lists low birth weight, small-for-gestational-age birth weight, neonatal respiratory distress, and early-onset asthma in childhood. ACOG's conclusion is direct: both chronic asthma and acute exacerbations remain undertreated in pregnancy.

This is not only a society opinion. The FDA-approved labels for common inhalers carry their own disease-risk statement. The Symbicort label says that in women with poorly or moderately controlled asthma, there is an increased risk of several perinatal adverse outcomes, such as preeclampsia in the mother and prematurity, low birth weight, and small for gestational age in the neonate. The Ventolin HFA label carries the same warning.

Preeclampsia is a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy, and blood pressure in pregnancy is tracked closely for that reason. Our sibling site hypertension.md covers blood pressure basics in more depth.

Which inhaled asthma medicines have the most pregnancy safety data?

Two names come up repeatedly because they have the largest human datasets.

  • Budesonide, an inhaled corticosteroid. Its label cites Swedish registry data covering roughly 99% of pregnancies from 1995 to 1997.
  • Albuterol (called salbutamol outside the United States), the standard quick-relief bronchodilator.

A 2024 review in Journal of Clinical Medicine on asthma treatment in pregnancy lists budesonide, beclomethasone, and fluticasone propionate as the preferred inhaled corticosteroids in pregnancy. It classes salbutamol as compatible with pregnancy and lactation, and the long-acting bronchodilators formoterol and salmeterol as probably safe. The same review notes that inhaled corticosteroid use reduces exacerbations in pregnancy, while stopping the medicine raises the risk.

ACOG goes further than "keep taking it." The 2025 guideline recommends a combination inhaler holding both an inhaled corticosteroid and a rapid-onset bronchodilator, used as needed and, in more persistent asthma, daily. That is a shift away from relying on a bronchodilator-only rescue inhaler. If that distinction is new to you, see our explainer on rescue inhalers versus controller inhalers.

What do the FDA labels actually say about inhalers in pregnancy?

First, the old letter grades are gone. The FDA's Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule took effect on June 30, 2015, and removed the pregnancy letter categories A, B, C, D, and X. Labels now carry narrative sections: 8.1 Pregnancy, 8.2 Lactation, and 8.3 Females and Males of Reproductive Potential.

Here is what the current text says for three common inhalers:

  • Albuterol (Ventolin HFA). There are no randomized clinical studies of albuterol use in pregnancy. Available data from epidemiologic studies and postmarketing case reports do not consistently demonstrate a risk of major birth defects or miscarriage. The label still frames use as a benefit-risk decision with a prescriber.
  • Budesonide (Pulmicort Flexhaler). The label reports no increased risk of congenital malformations from inhaled budesonide in early pregnancy. The recorded malformation rate was similar to the general population rate (3.8% versus 3.5%). An expanded analysis found 3.6%, matching the rate for all newborns in the same period.
  • Budesonide with formoterol (Symbicort). No adequate and well-controlled studies exist for the combination or for formoterol alone, but studies of inhaled budesonide alone have not shown increased risk of abnormalities.

No label instructs patients to stop an asthma inhaler because of pregnancy. Dosing is a decision for your prescriber, and nothing here replaces the instructions on your own label.

Does asthma get better or worse during pregnancy?

It changes often, and not in one direction. Older studies produced the familiar "rule of thirds," summarized in the NIH StatPearls chapter on asthma in pregnancy: roughly one third worsen, one third stay the same, and one third improve. A more recent cohort described in a 2025 review, Asthma and pregnancy, found asthma worsened in 40% of women and stayed the same in 60%.

Severity before pregnancy is the strongest predictor. That review reports exacerbation risk of 8% in mild asthma, 47% in moderate asthma, and 65% in severe asthma. About 20% of pregnant patients with asthma have an exacerbation that requires medication intervention.

Timing matters too. Exacerbations cluster in the second and third trimesters. One reason is behavioral: people stop their controller inhaler after learning they are pregnant, then lose control weeks later.

What monitoring should you expect across the trimesters?

Expect asthma control to be reviewed at roughly monthly intervals through the pregnancy, with medication adjusted by severity. Standard control questionnaires, such as the Asthma Control Test, are commonly used.

Breathing tests are not off limits. Spirometry has no contraindication in pregnancy, and monthly spirometry is described as safe in uncomplicated pregnancy. Bronchoprovocation (challenge) testing is not recommended. One point is worth remembering: FEV1, FVC, the FEV1/FVC ratio, and peak flow do not normally fall during pregnancy. A decline is a signal to act, not a normal finding to accept. Home tracking helps, and our guide explains what a peak flow meter reading tells you.

During an acute exacerbation, care shifts to the hospital pattern: oxygen targeted to a saturation above 95%, inhaled bronchodilators, early systemic corticosteroids, and fetal heart rate monitoring. ACOG states that exacerbations must be promptly recognized and treated. Having a written plan in hand shortens that delay, which is the point of an asthma attack action plan.

What happens to asthma care during labor and breastfeeding?

Usual maintenance inhalers continue through delivery. Exacerbations during labor itself are uncommon. A few specifics recur across sources:

  1. Beta-agonists can interfere with uterine contractility. The Ventolin HFA label restricts use during labor to patients in whom the benefits clearly outweigh the risk.
  2. Oxytocin is the drug of choice for induction and for postpartum hemorrhage. Prostaglandin F2-alpha derivatives can cause bronchoconstriction and are avoided.
  3. If high-dose quick-relief bronchodilator was used within 48 hours of delivery, newborn blood glucose is monitored, because neonatal hypoglycemia can occur.
  4. Regional anesthesia is preferred when anesthesia is needed.

Breastfeeding is encouraged, not restricted. The Pulmicort Flexhaler label states that the total daily oral dose of budesonide available to the infant in breast milk is approximately 0.3% to 1% of the dose the mother inhales. The Ventolin HFA label notes plasma albuterol levels after inhaled therapeutic doses are low in humans, so milk levels are likely correspondingly low. Inhaled corticosteroids and beta-agonists are not contraindicated during breastfeeding.

The bottom line

The safety question has been answered the same way by obstetric and respiratory groups for years: control the asthma. ACOG puts inhaled corticosteroids at the center of treatment for all pregnant patients with asthma and tells clinicians to actively discourage stopping or stepping down therapy during pregnancy. Budesonide and albuterol carry the most human data. FDA labels describe no consistent signal for major birth defects, and they spell out the risks of poorly controlled disease instead. Bring your inhaler list to your first prenatal visit and ask for a written plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are steroid tablets handled differently from inhalers when I am pregnant?

Yes, but they are not off the table. ACOG's 2025 guideline says asthma exacerbations in pregnancy must be promptly recognized and treated with systemic corticosteroids and bronchodilators. Reviews of the evidence describe short courses of oral corticosteroids as necessary for exacerbations, because uncontrolled severe asthma carries its own obstetric risks.

Can I start a biologic asthma drug while I am pregnant?

Starting one during pregnancy is generally not the plan. A 2024 review notes that omalizumab initiation during pregnancy is not recommended, and only continuation is advised for women already receiving it. All asthma biologics are IgG antibodies that cross the placenta to some degree, and human safety data outside omalizumab remain limited.

Does having asthma mean I will need a cesarean delivery?

No. A 2025 review states that asthma does not normally affect labor or the choice of delivery route. Cesarean delivery is more common among people with asthma, especially severe asthma, and ACOG lists it among the associated outcomes. That association reflects overall risk, not a rule about your delivery.

Why did the A, B, C, D, and X pregnancy categories disappear from my inhaler label?

The FDA removed them. The Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule took effect on June 30, 2015, and replaced the letter grades with narrative sections. Labels now describe the actual data, clinical considerations, and any pregnancy exposure registry under section 8.1, plus lactation data under 8.2.

What should I bring up at my first prenatal visit?

Bring the names of every inhaler and asthma medicine you use, including how often you reach for the quick-relief one. Ask whether your control is good enough and whether your regimen should change. Ask for a written action plan and for how often your breathing will be checked.
