Can Acid Reflux Make Asthma Worse?

September 3, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes, it can. Gastroesophageal reflux disease, or GERD, is far more common in people with asthma than in the general population. Acid in the lower esophagus can narrow the airways through a nerve reflex, and tiny amounts of stomach contents can reach the airways directly. But large trials show that treating silent reflux with acid blockers does not improve asthma. The link matters most when you also have reflux symptoms such as heartburn, night cough, or hoarseness.

How common is reflux in people with asthma?

Very common. A systematic review in the journal Gut pooled 28 studies on the two conditions. The average prevalence of GERD symptoms was 59.2% in people with asthma, compared with 38.1% in controls. Among people with asthma who had esophageal pH testing, 50.9% had abnormal acid exposure. The pooled odds ratio for GERD symptoms in asthma was 5.5.

The link runs the other way too, but it is weaker. The same review found asthma in 4.6% of people with GERD and 3.9% of controls, an odds ratio of 2.3. The authors noted a lack of data on which condition comes first.

Much of this reflux is silent. In the NIH-funded SARA trial, described below, pH monitoring documented reflux in 40% of adults who had minimal or no reflux symptoms. The GINA 2025 report adds a caution: part of the overlap may come from reflux cough being blamed on asthma.

How could stomach acid affect your airways?

Researchers describe two main pathways. A narrative review in PMC calls them the reflex theory and the reflux theory.

  • The vagal reflex. The esophagus and the airways share nerve supply through the vagus nerve. Acid in the lower esophagus stimulates that nerve, which can cause bronchoconstriction. The lungs react even though no acid reaches them.
  • Microaspiration. Refluxed material, including acid, pepsin, and bile acids, can travel up the esophagus and reach the airways in tiny amounts. This can provoke airway narrowing and inflammation directly.

Asthma can also feed reflux. When airways narrow, the chest works harder to pull air in. The review notes this creates a pressure gradient that helps stomach contents move upward. That is one reason the relationship is often called bidirectional.

Does treating reflux improve asthma?

For silent reflux, no. The SARA trial (Study of Acid Reflux in Asthma) was run by the American Lung Association Asthma Clinical Research Centers and funded by the National Institutes of Health. It enrolled 412 adults with poorly controlled asthma and minimal or no reflux symptoms. They took esomeprazole 40 mg twice a day or placebo for 24 weeks. Episodes of poor asthma control were nearly identical: 2.3 per person-year on placebo and 2.5 on esomeprazole. There was no benefit even in the 40% who had reflux on pH monitoring. Lung function, symptom scores, night waking, and quality of life did not improve either.

The same research network ran a trial in 306 children with poorly controlled asthma. Lansoprazole for 24 weeks improved neither symptoms nor lung function. Children on the drug had more respiratory infections, with a relative risk of 1.3.

What about people who do have reflux symptoms? A meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials with 2,524 adults found that proton pump inhibitors raised morning peak flow by 8.68 liters per minute (95% CI 2.35 to 15.02). Trials that required a GERD diagnosis showed a larger gain of 16.90 liters per minute. Symptom scores, quality of life, evening peak flow, and FEV1 did not change. The authors called the improvement unlikely to be clinically meaningful. For scale, see what a peak flow reading tells you.

GINA sums up the evidence this way. Symptomatic reflux should be treated. People with poorly controlled asthma should not receive anti-reflux therapy unless they also have reflux symptoms (Evidence A). Benefits appear limited to people with both symptomatic reflux and night-time respiratory symptoms.

Which symptoms suggest reflux is a factor?

Classic reflux symptoms come first. The NIDDK lists heartburn, regurgitation, chest pain, nausea, and trouble or pain with swallowing. It also lists chronic cough and hoarseness as signs of complications in the throat or lungs.

Certain asthma patterns raise suspicion too. The PMC narrative review says to consider GERD when asthma starts in adulthood, has no allergic component or family history, responds poorly to asthma medicines, or gets worse after meals. Night symptoms are a common clue. Lying down makes reflux easier, and GINA notes that a nocturnal cough on its own should prompt a look at rhinitis or GERD. Our guide to why asthma is worse at night covers the other overnight causes.

One caution from the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) GERD guideline: cough, hoarseness, throat clearing, and asthma have poor sensitivity and specificity for GERD. They can point to reflux, but they do not prove it. Chest pain and hoarseness overlap with many other conditions, so a symptom checker like symptom.md can help you sort what to raise with your doctor.

Can asthma medicines make reflux worse?

Some can. GINA states that beta2-agonists and theophylline relax the lower esophageal sphincter, the muscle valve that keeps acid in the stomach. An older study in Annals of Allergy found that intravenous aminophylline, a theophylline drug, produced a significant drop in sphincter pressure in both healthy and asthmatic volunteers. The PMC review cites a study linking theophylline to a 24% increase in daytime reflux.

The evidence on inhaled beta-agonists is mixed. The same review cites a 1991 study in which salbutamol did not change sphincter pressure. The AAAAI singles out theophylline as the medicine most likely to worsen reflux and suggests avoiding it when possible. The NIDDK also lists asthma medicines among drugs that can contribute to GERD.

Do not stop a controller inhaler because of heartburn. Inhaled corticosteroids are the foundation of asthma treatment, and reflux is not a reason to drop them. If you use a rescue inhaler often, that itself signals poor control. Compare the options in our guide to asthma inhalers and talk with your doctor about adjusting your plan.

What can you do, and when should you see a doctor?

Start with the lifestyle steps that the ACG guideline and MedlinePlus recommend for reflux:

  • Avoid meals within 2 to 3 hours of bedtime, and do not lie down right after eating.
  • Raise the head of your bed if symptoms hit at night.
  • Lose weight if you are overweight. The ACG gives this a strong recommendation.
  • Stop smoking and avoid secondhand smoke.
  • Eat smaller meals and skip foods that set off your symptoms, such as fatty or spicy foods, chocolate, and caffeine.

Then match the next step to your symptoms. If you have heartburn or regurgitation plus asthma or cough, the ACG suggests a doctor may consider an 8 to 12 week trial of twice-daily proton pump inhibitor therapy. If you have cough, hoarseness, or asthma without heartburn or regurgitation, the ACG recommends reflux testing before starting a PPI. GINA agrees there is no value in screening everyone with uncontrolled asthma for GERD.

See a doctor if reflux symptoms happen more than occasionally, if your asthma stays uncontrolled despite correct inhaler use, or if you have trouble swallowing, pain with swallowing, or a hoarse voice that lasts. Get emergency help for any red-zone asthma symptoms, no matter what you think is causing them.

The bottom line

Reflux and asthma travel together. About 6 in 10 people with asthma report GERD symptoms, and half of those tested have abnormal acid exposure. Acid can tighten the airways through the vagus nerve or through microaspiration, and some asthma medicines loosen the valve that holds acid down. Even so, acid blockers do not fix asthma in people without reflux symptoms. The SARA trial and a pediatric trial both showed no benefit, and the meta-analysis gain in morning peak flow was too small to matter. Treat reflux when you have reflux symptoms. Treat asthma with asthma medicine. If night cough, post-meal flares, or hoarseness are part of your picture, ask your doctor to look at both.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you have reflux without heartburn?

Yes. In the SARA trial, pH monitoring found reflux in 40% of adults with poorly controlled asthma who had minimal or no reflux symptoms. This is often called silent reflux. The trial also showed that treating silent reflux with a proton pump inhibitor did not improve asthma control.

Should everyone with uncontrolled asthma be tested for GERD?

No. The GINA 2025 report says there is no value in screening people with uncontrolled asthma for GERD, and it rates that as Evidence A. Testing or an empirical trial of reflux medicine makes sense when you have reflux symptoms such as heartburn or regurgitation.

Will a proton pump inhibitor help my asthma if I do have heartburn?

It may help the heartburn, and it might nudge morning peak flow upward. A meta-analysis of 11 trials found an average gain of 8.68 liters per minute, larger in trials limited to people with GERD. Symptoms, quality of life, and FEV1 did not change. GINA notes that benefits appear limited to people with both symptomatic reflux and night-time respiratory symptoms.

Is theophylline still used for asthma?

It is used much less than in the past, and inhaled corticosteroid-based treatment is the standard today. Theophylline relaxes the lower esophageal sphincter and has been linked to more reflux. The AAAAI suggests avoiding it when possible if you have GERD. Ask your doctor before changing any asthma medicine.

Does reflux cause asthma, or does asthma cause reflux?

Both directions have support, and the direction is not settled. The Gut systematic review found a stronger link from asthma to GERD symptoms (odds ratio 5.5) than from GERD to asthma (odds ratio 2.3). Airway narrowing can also pull stomach contents upward by changing chest pressure, so the two conditions can reinforce each other.
