What Happens During a Methacholine Challenge Test for Asthma?

October 3, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: You breathe in a mist of methacholine in rising strengths and blow into a spirometer after each one. Methacholine makes sensitive airways tighten. The test is positive if your FEV1 falls by 20% or more, and it stops at that point. You then get a reliever inhaler, and staff keep you until your breathing is back near its starting level.

Why is a methacholine challenge ordered?

Doctors order it when symptoms suggest asthma but the usual breathing tests look normal. Asthma narrows the airways at some times and not at others. A clinic test on a good day can miss it. Our post on asthma with normal spirometry explains that problem and the tests that usually come first.

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute describes challenge tests as a way to check how sensitive your airways are. Staff compare how fast you can blow air out before and after a challenge agent, such as methacholine.

The FDA label for Provocholine (methacholine chloride) says people with asthma are more sensitive to methacholine than healthy people. That difference is the basis of the test.

The test also helps when a cough is the only symptom. The GINA 2026 summary guide says cough-variant asthma shows airway hyperresponsiveness on bronchial challenge testing, even when the bronchodilator test is negative. A long-lasting cough has many other causes. symptom.md covers unexplained cough and breathlessness.

How do you prepare for the test?

Some medicines blunt the airway response and can hide a positive result. The Provocholine label lists how long each one is held before the test:

  • Short-acting beta-agonists (for example, albuterol): 6 hours.
  • Long-acting beta-agonists (for example, salmeterol): 36 hours.
  • Short-acting anticholinergics (for example, ipratropium): 12 hours.
  • Long-acting anticholinergics (for example, tiotropium): 168 hours or more, which is 7 days.
  • Oral theophylline: 12 to 48 hours.

The label says oral or inhaled corticosteroids may be continued. It notes that regular use can lower the airway response to methacholine. Many combination inhalers contain a long-acting beta-agonist and a steroid together, so ask which rule applies to yours. See rescue versus controller inhalers if you are unsure.

Do not stop a medicine on your own. Your lab will give you a written list, and its times may differ from the label. If you need your reliever during the hold period, use it and tell the lab.

Tell the lab about a recent cold or flu, a recent vaccine, and smoking. The label lists each of these as a cause of an occasional false positive result. Tell staff if you take a beta-blocker. The label warns that beta-blockers may make the airway tightening harder to reverse.

For lung function tests in general, MedlinePlus advises loose clothing and no large meal within 2 hours. Your provider may also ask you to avoid caffeine, smoking, alcohol, and heavy exercise.

What happens step by step?

The label says the test must take place in a pulmonary function laboratory or clinic, run by trained staff. Emergency medicine and equipment must be immediately available. The steps on the label are:

  1. Baseline spirometry. You take a full breath in and blow out hard into a mouthpiece. This gives your starting FEV1, the volume of air you force out in one second.
  2. A dose with no drug. You inhale a mist of salt water or a base solution with no methacholine in it. Staff measure your FEV1 30 and 90 seconds later. Later readings are compared with this value.
  3. The first methacholine dose. You inhale the weakest strength from a nebulizer, with a nose clip on. The label describes two methods. In one, you breathe quietly for 1 minute. In the other, you take five slow, deep breaths and hold each one for about 5 seconds.
  4. Spirometry again. Staff measure your FEV1 30 and 90 seconds after the dose.
  5. Stronger doses. Each step doubles or quadruples the strength, from 0.0625 mg/mL up to 16 mg/mL. That makes at most five steps on the quadrupling schedule, or nine on the doubling schedule.
  6. The stop point. Dosing ends when your FEV1 has fallen by 20% or more, or when you have had the highest strength, whichever comes first.

Labs differ on the breathing method. The 2017 European Respiratory Society technical standard recommends quiet tidal breathing for 1 minute or more, or a dosimeter with a suitable breath count. It advises against methods that use maximal deep breaths, because a deep breath protects the airways and makes the test less sensitive.

What do positive and negative results mean?

The Provocholine label defines a positive test as a fall in FEV1 of 20% or more from the post-diluent value. A negative (normal) test is a fall of less than 20% after all the doses.

The report also states how much methacholine it took to reach that 20% fall. Older reports give a concentration, called PC20, in mg/mL. The ERS standard changed this. It bases the result on the delivered dose, called PD20, so that results from different devices can be compared.

A 2019 review of the test by Donald Cockcroft, a co-author of the ERS standard, summarizes how to read the PD20:

  • PD20 above 400 micrograms: the test is negative. When symptoms are current, this excludes current asthma with reasonable certainty.
  • PD20 above 25 and up to 400 micrograms: asthma is possible. The lower the PD20, and the more your symptoms fit asthma, the more likely it is.
  • PD20 of 25 micrograms or less: highly specific for asthma. The review notes that this cut-off has low sensitivity, so many people with asthma score above it.

The test is better at ruling asthma out than at proving it. A positive result does not always mean asthma. The label says a false positive can occur after influenza or an upper respiratory infection, after immunizations, and in smokers. It can also occur with allergic rhinitis or chronic lung disease such as COPD.

Is the test safe, and who should not have it?

The label lists bronchospasm as the main adverse reaction, with chest tightness, cough, or wheezing. Less common reactions are headache, throat irritation, light-headedness, and itching.

The label carries a boxed warning. Severe bronchoconstriction can result from methacholine, including at the lowest dose. The label says the following people should not have the test:

  • Anyone with a baseline FEV1 below 60% of predicted, and adults with an FEV1 below 1.5 L.
  • Anyone with a past hypersensitivity reaction to methacholine or similar drugs.

The label does not recommend the test for people with clinically apparent asthma or wheezing. It also does not recommend it for people with uncontrolled high blood pressure, an aortic aneurysm, or a history of heart attack or stroke.

Pregnancy is a separate case. The label does not recommend bronchial challenge testing in pregnant women because of the potential for low oxygen in the fetus. GINA gives the same advice and says to confirm the diagnosis after delivery. Our guide to asthma inhalers during pregnancy covers treatment in the meantime.

How long does it take, and what happens after?

The label gives no total time for the visit. A test that turns positive at a low strength ends early. A negative test runs through every strength. Ask your lab how long to allow.

After the last dose, staff give you an inhaled beta-agonist, such as albuterol. They wait 10 minutes and measure your lung function again. The label says most people return to normal lung function within 10 to 20 minutes of the bronchodilator. Without one, most people recover in 30 to 45 minutes.

You cannot leave until your FEV1 is back within 90% of your baseline. Ask the lab when to restart the medicines you held.

The bottom line

A methacholine challenge is a stepwise breathing test in a specialist lab. You inhale rising strengths of methacholine and repeat spirometry after each one. A fall in FEV1 of 20% or more is positive. A negative test, with a PD20 above 400 micrograms, makes current asthma unlikely. A positive test supports asthma but does not prove it alone. Follow the lab's list of medicines to hold, and tell staff about recent infections and pregnancy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a methacholine challenge test hurt?

It does not hurt, but you may feel chest tightness, cough, or wheezing as the dose rises. The FDA label lists these as symptoms of bronchospasm, the main adverse reaction. Staff give you a reliever inhaler at the end, and most people recover within 10 to 20 minutes.

Can I use my albuterol inhaler on the morning of the test?

The Provocholine label says short-acting beta-agonists such as albuterol are held for 6 hours before the test. If you need your reliever during that time, use it and tell the lab. The test can be moved to another day.

Do I have to stop my steroid inhaler before the test?

The label says oral or inhaled corticosteroids may be continued. Many combination inhalers also contain a long-acting beta-agonist, which the label says to hold for 36 hours. Ask your lab which rule applies to your inhaler.

Can a positive methacholine test be wrong?

Yes. The label says a false positive can occur after influenza or an upper respiratory infection, after immunizations, in smokers, and in people with allergic rhinitis or chronic lung disease. Your clinician reads the result together with your symptoms and other tests.

Can children have a methacholine challenge test?

The FDA label covers children aged 5 years and older who do not have clinically apparent asthma. Safety and effectiveness are not established in children younger than 5. A child's baseline FEV1 must be at least 60% of the predicted value.
