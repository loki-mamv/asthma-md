Do You Really Need A Spacer With A Metered-Dose Inhaler?

September 28, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Not always, but most people who use a metered-dose inhaler benefit from one. A spacer removes the need to time your breath with the puff, and it cuts the amount of medicine that lands in your mouth and throat. The Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) says inhaled steroids given by metered-dose inhaler should preferably be used with a spacer, and that children should always use one. If you use a dry powder inhaler, you do not need a spacer at all.

What does a spacer actually do?

A spacer is a tube or chamber that attaches to the mouthpiece of a pressurized metered-dose inhaler (MDI). You spray the puff into the chamber, then breathe it in from the other end. A valved holding chamber is a spacer with a one-way valve, so the medicine stays in the chamber until you inhale.

A 2004 review of spacer devices describes a range from small tubes under 50 mL to holding chambers of 750 mL. Compared with an MDI alone, the review found that spacers:

  • Reduce coordination problems, because you no longer need to press and inhale at the same instant.
  • Reduce the medicine deposited in the mouth and throat.
  • Often, but not always, increase the amount that reaches the lungs.

The spray from an MDI leaves the canister fast. Without a spacer, much of it hits the back of the throat. MedlinePlus puts it plainly: with poor technique, less medicine gets to your lungs and most stays in the back of your mouth.

What do asthma guidelines say about spacers?

The 2025 GINA report makes several direct statements about spacers:

  • For anyone prescribed an MDI, a spacer improves delivery of the medicine to the lungs.
  • Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) by MDI should preferably be used with a spacer.
  • For ICS-formoterol given by MDI, a spacer is recommended.
  • Dry powder inhalers are not suitable for most children aged 5 and under and some older adults, so MDIs with spacers remain essential for them.

GINA also stresses that technique matters more than the device alone. Checking inhaler technique with a checklist takes 2 to 3 minutes and improves asthma control. Technique slips over time, so a clinician or pharmacist should recheck it regularly. If you are not sure which device you have, our guide to asthma inhalers explains the main types.

The NHLBI metered-dose inhaler instructions treat a spacer as optional for adults: "If you use a spacer, put the inhaler in the rubber ring on the end of the spacer." MedlinePlus is more direct: if you have a spacer, use it.

Does a spacer get more medicine into your lungs?

It depends on your technique and on the spacer. Two imaging studies from the same research group show the range.

In a 1998 study in healthy volunteers using good technique, about 11.0% of a fenoterol dose reached the lungs from an MDI alone and 9.9% with an AeroChamber spacer. Lung delivery was about the same. But about 71.7% of the dose from the MDI alone landed in the mouth and throat.

In a 2001 study in 12 adults with mild to moderate asthma, budesonide given by MDI through a 750 mL Nebuhaler spacer reached the lungs at 44.1% of the dose. Two dry powder inhalers delivered 18.5% and 21.8%. The spacer also cut throat deposition compared with both powder devices.

The practical lesson matches the 2004 review. Spacers may not improve results for people who already use an MDI perfectly. Many people do not, and a spacer covers the most common error: pressing the canister at the wrong moment. For timing between puffs, see how long to wait between inhaler puffs.

Does a spacer help prevent thrush from steroid inhalers?

Yes. GINA lists oral thrush (oropharyngeal candidiasis) and hoarse voice (dysphonia) as the main local side effects of inhaled steroids. It says both can be reduced by using a spacer with an MDI and by rinsing with water and spitting after each dose.

The reason is simple. Thrush grows where steroid settles in the mouth and throat. A spacer holds back the fast, large particles that would otherwise land there. The NHLBI sheet adds that rinsing after an inhaled corticosteroid helps prevent infection in the mouth. Our article on whether a steroid inhaler can cause oral thrush covers treatment and other ways to lower the risk.

Should children and people having an attack use a spacer?

For young children, the answer is yes. GINA states that in children, an MDI should always be used with a spacer. For children 5 and under, the preferred device is an MDI with a valved spacer:

  • Ages 0 to 3: spacer with a face mask.
  • Ages 4 to 5: spacer with a mouthpiece.

Young children breathe normally through the spacer after each puff. GINA says 5 to 10 breaths per puff are generally enough. Give one puff at a time, and start the breaths right after the puff. MedlinePlus notes that almost all children can use a spacer. The FDA label for Ventolin HFA adds that its safety and effectiveness with or without a spacer in children under 4 have not been shown. For more on devices by age, see our parents' guide to childhood asthma.

During an attack, a spacer works as well as a nebulizer for most people. A 2013 Cochrane review of 39 trials with 1,897 children and 729 adults found no significant difference in hospital admission between the two. Children treated with a spacer left the emergency department about 33 minutes sooner and had less tremor. GINA calls MDI plus spacer the most cost-effective route for reliever medicine, provided the person can use it. Follow your written asthma action plan for doses. If a rescue inhaler does not help, get emergency care. If you are unsure whether your breathing symptoms are asthma, symptom.md covers common causes of wheeze and breathlessness.

How do you use and clean a spacer?

Always follow the instructions that come with your inhaler and spacer. The general steps from MedlinePlus are below. The CDC also offers short videos and printed sheets on using an MDI with a spacer.

  1. Prime the inhaler if needed, remove the cap, and shake it 10 to 15 times.
  2. Attach the spacer and breathe out fully.
  3. Seal your lips around the spacer mouthpiece.
  4. Spray one puff into the spacer, then breathe in slowly and deeply.
  5. Hold your breath for a count of 10, if you can, then breathe out slowly.
  6. For quick-relief medicine, wait before the next puff. Rinse and spit after a steroid.

Spray only one puff at a time. GINA warns that several puffs into the chamber at once can markedly reduce the dose you inhale.

For cleaning, the NHLBI says never put the medicine canister in water and never brush or wipe the inside of the spacer. Plastic spacers build up static charge, which pulls medicine onto the walls. GINA says washing with detergent, without rinsing, and letting the spacer air dry reduces that charge. A new plastic spacer kept for emergencies should be washed this way regularly, for example monthly. Do not share a spacer with family members.

Spacers are made only for MDIs. Dry powder inhalers rely on your own quick, deep breath to pull the powder out, so they are used without a spacer. Soft mist inhalers such as Respimat release a slower mist. In the 1998 study, Respimat delivered 39.2% of the fenoterol dose to the lungs.

The bottom line

You can use a metered-dose inhaler without a spacer if your technique is very good. Most people get more reliable dosing and less medicine in the throat with one. A spacer is strongly advised for inhaled steroids, for ICS-formoterol by MDI, and always for children. It works as well as a nebulizer during most attacks. Ask your pharmacist or clinician to check your technique and recommend a spacer that fits your inhaler.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a spacer with a dry powder inhaler?

No. Spacers are made for pressurized metered-dose inhalers. Dry powder inhalers work by your own fast, deep breath pulling the powder straight from the device, so they are used on their own.

Is a spacer the same as a valved holding chamber?

A valved holding chamber is a type of spacer with a one-way valve. The valve keeps the medicine in the chamber until you breathe in, which helps young children who breathe in and out through it. GINA advises checking that the valve moves while a child breathes through it.

Do I need a separate spacer for each inhaler?

Many spacers fit most standard metered-dose inhalers, but check the instructions for your devices. Do not share a spacer with other people, because it can spread infection. Ask your pharmacist whether your spacer fits each inhaler you use.

How often should a spacer be replaced?

Follow the manufacturer's instructions, since the lifespan varies between products. Replace it if it cracks or the valve stops moving, and GINA notes a face mask must fit tightly. A pharmacist can check whether yours still works properly.

Can I give a child several puffs into the spacer at once to save time?

No. GINA advises one puff at a time, with the inhaler shaken before each puff. Several puffs into the chamber at once can markedly reduce the amount of medicine the child inhales.
