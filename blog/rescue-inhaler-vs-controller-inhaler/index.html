What Is the Difference Between a Rescue Inhaler and a Controller Inhaler?

September 8, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: A rescue inhaler opens tight airways within minutes and wears off in hours. A controller inhaler treats the swelling underneath, so it is taken on a schedule whether you feel fine or not. Albuterol is the classic rescue drug. Inhaled steroids (ICS) are the backbone of control. Since 2019, guidelines have moved away from rescue-only treatment, and newer "anti-inflammatory relievers" put a small steroid dose into every rescue puff.

What does a rescue inhaler do?

A rescue inhaler holds a bronchodilator. It relaxes the muscle wrapped around your airways so air can move again. The NHLBI lists inhaled short-acting beta2-agonists (SABAs) as the main quick-relief medicine, with short-acting anticholinergics as a backup for people who get side effects from SABAs.

Albuterol is the SABA most people carry. MedlinePlus names albuterol (ProAir HFA, Proventil HFA, Ventolin HFA) and levalbuterol (Xopenex HFA). The FDA label for albuterol gives the standard dose as 2 inhalations every 4 to 6 hours. Common side effects include shakiness, a fast heartbeat, headache, and restlessness.

Two lines on that label matter more than the dose. "Need for more doses of albuterol sulfate inhalation aerosol than usual may be a sign of deterioration of asthma and requires reevaluation of treatment." And: "Albuterol sulfate is not a substitute for corticosteroids." Albuterol treats the symptom. It does nothing for the cause.

What does a controller inhaler do?

Asthma is airway inflammation. A controller treats that inflammation so attacks happen less often. The MedlinePlus control drug page puts it plainly: "You must use these medicines every day for them to work as well as possible," even on days with no symptoms.

The main controller classes, per the ACAAI and NHLBI:

  • Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS). Fluticasone, budesonide, mometasone, beclomethasone, and ciclesonide. ACAAI calls them "among the most effective medications" for asthma.
  • ICS plus a long-acting beta-agonist (ICS-LABA). Salmeterol, formoterol, or vilanterol paired with a steroid. LABAs are "never taken alone." MedlinePlus repeats the warning: use them only with an inhaled steroid.
  • Leukotriene modifiers (LTRA). Montelukast, zafirlukast, and zileuton, taken as pills.
  • Long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA). An add-on option. The NHLBI 2020 update prefers adding a LABA over a LAMA for most people.
  • Biologics. Injected drugs such as benralizumab for asthma that stays uncontrolled on inhalers.

What is an anti-inflammatory reliever?

The line between rescue and control has blurred on purpose. The GINA 2026 strategy report explains why low-dose ICS-formoterol "is called an anti-inflammatory reliever (AIR) because it relieves symptoms and reduces inflammation." Formoterol opens the airway as fast as albuterol, and the budesonide in the same puff treats the swelling. GINA now defines "controller" as any ICS-containing medicine, including one taken only when symptoms occur.

GINA lays out two tracks. In Track 1, the reliever is as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol. In Track 2, the reliever is ICS-SABA, or plain SABA if that is not available, alongside daily controller medicine. Track 1 is preferred. In mild asthma, as-needed ICS-formoterol cut severe attacks and emergency visits "by about two-thirds, compared with SABA-only treatment."

The trial data back that up. In the Novel START trial, 668 adults with mild asthma were followed for 52 weeks. Yearly exacerbation rates were 0.195 with as-needed budesonide-formoterol versus 0.400 with as-needed albuterol (relative rate 0.49; 95% CI 0.33 to 0.72). Severe attacks: 9 versus 23. A meta-analysis of four trials found the same pattern, with severe exacerbations cut by more than half compared with SABA alone (RR 0.46; 95% CI 0.36 to 0.59).

The United States has its own version. In January 2023 the FDA approved Airsupra (albuterol and budesonide), "the first product containing an ICS to be approved in the U.S. as a reliever treatment (rather than as a controller) for asthma." It is for adults 18 and older. Per the Airsupra label, the dose is 2 inhalations as needed, with no more than 6 doses (12 inhalations) in 24 hours. It "is not to be used as maintenance treatment." In the MANDALA trial it lowered the risk of a severe attack by 28% compared with albuterol alone (HR 0.72; 95% CI 0.60 to 0.86).

For people age 4 and up with moderate to severe persistent asthma, the NHLBI 2020 update says the preferred treatment is one ICS-formoterol inhaler "used as both a daily asthma controller and quick-relief therapy." Doctors call this SMART. One inhaler, two jobs.

Why is a rescue inhaler alone no longer recommended?

For decades, people with mild asthma got an albuterol inhaler and nothing else. GINA no longer recommends that at any step, and gives three reasons.

  1. The risk data. GINA reports that dispensing 3 or more 200-dose SABA canisters in a year "is associated with increased risk of exacerbations," and 12 or more canisters a year "is associated with markedly increased risk of death."
  2. Receptor down-regulation. Regular SABA use "causes beta-receptor down-regulation and reduction in response," which pushes people to use more.
  3. Nothing treats the inflammation. Daily ICS works well when people take it. GINA says adherence "in the community is poor," so many patients end up on SABA alone anyway.

A large Canadian study, SABINA Canada, shows the size of the problem. Among 115,478 patients, people who filled 3 or more SABA canisters a year had more severe exacerbations. In Alberta the yearly rate was 0.31 versus 0.17. After adjustment, overuse raised the risk by 32% in Alberta (IRR 1.32; 95% CI 1.27 to 1.38) and 36% in Nova Scotia (IRR 1.36; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.56).

How can you tell which inhaler is which?

Do not rely on color. Read the drug name on the canister or the pharmacy label instead:

  • Rescue (SABA): albuterol or levalbuterol. You feel it work in minutes.
  • Anti-inflammatory reliever: budesonide-formoterol, or albuterol-budesonide (Airsupra). Fast relief plus a steroid.
  • Controller: fluticasone, budesonide, mometasone, beclomethasone, or ciclesonide alone, or paired with salmeterol, vilanterol, or formoterol. You feel nothing right away.

Two more clues. A rescue inhaler is prescribed "as needed." A controller is prescribed "twice daily" or "once daily." And if your one inhaler is budesonide-formoterol prescribed both ways, you are on SMART. Ask your doctor to write the plan down. Your asthma action plan should name each inhaler and say when to use it.

How much rescue inhaler use is too much?

The CDC defines frequent use as more than 2 days per week. Frequent use "may indicate inadequate asthma control and the need to initiate or intensify anti-inflammatory medications." In CDC survey data, 24.3% of adults and 18.7% of children with active asthma reported frequent use. ACAAI adds two more warning signs: waking with symptoms two or more nights a month, and refilling rescue medicine more than two times a year.

Count canisters. If you go through 3 in a year, GINA says your risk of a severe attack is already higher. That is the signal to see your doctor, not to buy another canister. Nighttime symptoms count too. Our guide on why asthma is worse at night explains what those wake-ups mean.

What to bring to that visit: your rescue use per week, your refill dates, and peak flow readings if you have them. If shortness of breath has other causes in your case, symptom.md can help you sort what to report. If your rescue inhaler stops helping during an attack, get emergency care.

The bottom line

A rescue inhaler opens the airway now. A controller keeps the airway from closing later. Albuterol alone treats symptoms and leaves the inflammation untouched, which is why 3 or more canisters a year raises your risk of a severe attack. Guidelines now prefer a reliever that contains an inhaled steroid, such as budesonide-formoterol or Airsupra, or a daily controller with rescue medicine on the side. Read the drug name, not the color, and track how often you reach for the rescue inhaler. More than 2 days a week means your asthma plan needs a change.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use my controller inhaler during an asthma attack?

Only if it contains formoterol and your doctor has told you to use it that way. Formoterol works fast, so budesonide-formoterol can serve as both controller and reliever under a SMART plan. Fluticasone-salmeterol and other non-formoterol combinations act too slowly to rescue you. Use the reliever your action plan names.

Is Airsupra a rescue inhaler or a controller?

It is a rescue inhaler with a steroid in it. The FDA approved Airsupra (albuterol and budesonide) in January 2023 for as-needed use in adults 18 and older. Its label says it is not to be used as maintenance treatment. The dose is 2 inhalations as needed, and no more than 6 doses in 24 hours.

Why do I not feel anything when I use my controller inhaler?

Because inhaled steroids reduce swelling over days and weeks, not minutes. There is no immediate sensation, which leads many people to stop taking them. Keep using the controller every day as prescribed, even when you feel well. Fewer attacks and less rescue inhaler use are the signs it is working.

How many albuterol canisters a year is safe?

GINA reports that dispensing 3 or more 200-dose canisters a year is tied to a higher risk of severe attacks, and 12 or more a year to a markedly higher risk of death. Two or fewer is the range GINA compares against. If you are refilling more than twice a year, ask your doctor about an ICS-containing reliever or a stronger controller.

Should someone with mild asthma still take a controller?

Current guidelines say people with mild asthma should not rely on albuterol alone. GINA's preferred option is an as-needed budesonide-formoterol inhaler, which acts as a controller and a reliever. A daily low-dose inhaled steroid with a separate rescue inhaler is the alternative. Either is safer than a rescue inhaler by itself.
