Why Does My Asthma Get Worse When the Weather Changes?

August 26, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Weather changes hit asthma through a few separate routes. Cold, dry air pulls heat and water out of the airway lining, and that alone can raise airway resistance by 40% to 49% in people with asthma. Humid or stormy air carries more pollen, mold, and dust mite allergen, and thunderstorms can shatter pollen into particles small enough to reach the small airways. Heat brings ozone and wildfire smoke. Barometric pressure gets blamed often, but the evidence for it is weak. Each route has a practical countermeasure.

Why does cold, dry air tighten my airways?

Your nose and upper airway warm and humidify every breath before it reaches the lungs. Cold winter air is also dry air, so the airway lining loses heat and water fast. The fluid on the airway surface becomes more concentrated. That change triggers mast cells to release histamine, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins, and the smooth muscle around the airway contracts. The NIH StatPearls review of exercise-induced bronchoconstriction describes this osmotic mechanism in detail. It also notes that this reaction affects 40% to 90% of people with asthma.

You do not need to run to feel it. In a study in the European Respiratory Journal, people breathed -5°C dry air through the nose at rest. Airway resistance rose 31% in people without asthma. It rose 40% in people with asthma and 49% in people with asthma plus rhinitis. Cold receptors in the nose appear to trigger a reflex that narrows the lower airways.

How does humidity change my symptoms?

Humid air feels heavy, but the bigger problem is what grows in it. The EPA asthma triggers page lists mold and dust mites as two of the most common home triggers. Both thrive in damp air. The EPA recommends keeping indoor relative humidity between 30% and 50%. A cheap hygrometer tells you where you stand. Wet items should dry within one to two days, and leaks need fixing promptly.

Humidity swings matter too, even within one day. A two-year study of 25,401 pediatric asthma emergency visits found that a 10% rise in humidity within a day predicted about one extra emergency visit on each of the next two days. For the full list of everyday triggers, see common asthma triggers.

What is thunderstorm asthma?

On 21 November 2016, a storm crossed Melbourne, Australia, during peak grass pollen season. Pollen counts were above 100 grains per cubic meter. The gust front dropped the temperature by 10°C and pushed humidity above 70%. Within 30 hours, emergency departments saw 3,365 excess respiratory presentations, a 672% increase. Hospitals recorded 476 excess asthma admissions, a 992% increase. Ten people died. Those figures come from the Lancet Planetary Health investigation of the event. Only 28% of surveyed patients had current doctor-diagnosed asthma. Most had hay fever and did not know they were at risk.

The mechanism is pollen rupture. Intact ryegrass pollen measures 30 to 40 micrometers, too large to pass the nose. When grains rupture, each one releases about 700 sub-pollen particles sized 0.6 to 2.5 micrometers. Those particles reach the small airways. A PLOS One modeling study found that humidity alone did not explain the Melbourne event, because relative humidity was only 18% before the storm. Lightning and wind friction likely ruptured the grains as well. Air sampling during the event showed ruptured grass pollen counts rose 250% when the storm outflow arrived.

A 2019 review in the Journal of Asthma and Allergy lists the risk factors: allergic rhinitis, ryegrass pollen sensitization, existing asthma, poor adherence to inhaled corticosteroid preventer therapy, a hospital admission in the past year, and being outdoors when the storm hits. If you have both hay fever and asthma, read how asthma and allergies connect.

Do temperature swings and barometric pressure matter?

Temperature swings have solid evidence. A Hong Kong study covering 2004 to 2011 found that each 1°C increase in the daily temperature range raised emergency asthma admissions by 2.49%. The effect was stronger in the cool season and in children. The pediatric emergency study above found the same pattern. A 10°F change in temperature from one day to the next predicted 1.8 additional emergency visits.

Barometric pressure is a different story. Many people feel their chest tighten before a front moves through, but the studies do not back that up. The 25,401-visit pediatric study reported no association with barometric pressure changes. A Hong Kong analysis of childhood asthma admissions found no link to atmospheric pressure either. A front usually brings temperature, humidity, and pollen changes at the same time. Those are the likely culprits, not the pressure reading itself.

How do heat, ozone, and wildfire smoke affect asthma?

Ground-level ozone forms when sunlight cooks vehicle and industrial emissions. The EPA says ozone reaches unhealthy levels especially on hot, sunny days. It aggravates asthma and increases the frequency of attacks. People with asthma, children, and anyone active outdoors face the highest risk. In the EPA clinician course on ozone, one cited study found clinic visits for childhood asthma were about one-third higher the day after ozone reached 0.11 ppm.

Wildfire smoke is mostly fine particulate matter. The EPA particle pollution page explains that particles under 10 micrometers get deep into the lungs, and some enter the bloodstream. Aggravated asthma is a listed effect. Indoors, the EPA wildfire smoke course recommends a MERV 13 or higher furnace filter run continuously, which cuts indoor PM2.5 by about 50%. A portable air cleaner on its highest setting in one closed room works too.

What can I do when the weather changes?

The steps map to the mechanisms above.

  • Cover your mouth and nose in the cold. A scarf or a heat-and-moisture-exchange mask warms and humidifies air before it reaches the airway. In a 2020 trial of 26 people with asthma cycling at 8°C, lung function fell 13% with no mask and 6% with the mask. An earlier study at -10°C saw FEV1 drop 27% untreated, 12% with a mask, 7% with a pre-exercise bronchodilator, and 0% with both.
  • Use your reliever before exercise, per your action plan. The GINA 2025 summary guide says patients at any step can take their reliever before exercise if needed. Track 1 patients can take low-dose ICS-formoterol before exercise or before pollen exposure.
  • Keep taking your controller. GINA reports that daily inhaled corticosteroid halves the risk of severe attacks compared with a rescue inhaler alone, even in people with symptoms one day per week or less. Adults and adolescents should not be treated with SABA alone. Poor controller adherence was a named risk factor in the Melbourne deaths.
  • Watch pollen and thunderstorm alerts. Victoria's health department now runs a thunderstorm asthma forecast from 1 October to 31 December each year. In the US, check the Air Quality Index and local pollen counts. Stay indoors with windows closed before and during a storm in pollen season.
  • Control indoor humidity. Hold it between 30% and 50% with a dehumidifier or air conditioning. Wash bedding weekly in hot water for dust mites.
  • Have a written plan. GINA says every patient should have a written asthma action plan. Ours is at asthma attack action plan.

One more point. Breathlessness in cold air can trigger a panic response, and panic makes breathing faster and shallower. If you cannot tell whether an episode is asthma or anxiety, use your peak flow meter and your action plan. The overlap is covered at anxiety.md.

The bottom line

Weather does not change your asthma by magic. Cold air dries and cools the airway lining. Humidity feeds mold and mites. Thunderstorms shatter pollen. Heat makes ozone, and fires make smoke. Temperature swings of a few degrees measurably raise hospital admissions. Barometric pressure on its own probably does not. Cover your face in the cold, pre-treat before exercise, watch the pollen and air quality forecasts, and take your controller every day.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can weather changes cause an asthma attack in someone who has never had asthma?

Yes, in rare storm events. In the 2016 Melbourne thunderstorm asthma event, only 28% of surveyed patients had current doctor-diagnosed asthma. Most of the rest had hay fever and grass pollen allergy. If you have hay fever, ask your doctor whether you need a reliever inhaler during pollen season.

Is a scarf over my mouth as good as a special cold-weather mask?

A scarf traps some warmth and moisture, and it costs nothing. Heat-and-moisture-exchange masks are designed for this job and performed well in trials, cutting the post-exercise drop in lung function roughly in half. Either option beats breathing cold air directly through the mouth. Breathing through your nose helps too.

Should I use a humidifier or a dehumidifier for asthma?

It depends on your reading, not the season. The EPA recommends 30% to 50% indoor relative humidity. Above 50%, dust mites and mold grow. Below 30%, airway linings dry out. Measure first with a hygrometer, then correct in whichever direction you need.

Why do I feel worse the day after a hot, sunny day?

Ozone and its effects lag. EPA clinician material cites a study in which childhood asthma clinic visits were about one-third higher the day after ozone reached 0.11 ppm. Ozone also primes the airways to react more strongly to allergens. Check the Air Quality Index before outdoor exercise in summer.

Does my inhaler still matter if my asthma only flares in bad weather?

Yes. GINA 2025 reports that daily inhaled corticosteroid halves the risk of severe attacks even in people with symptoms one day per week or less. Poor adherence to preventer therapy was a risk factor in the Melbourne thunderstorm deaths. Weather triggers hit uncontrolled airways much harder than controlled ones.
