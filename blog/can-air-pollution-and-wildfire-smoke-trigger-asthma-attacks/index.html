Can Air Pollution and Wildfire Smoke Trigger Asthma Attacks?

September 6, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes. Fine particles (PM2.5) and ozone both inflame the airways, and people with asthma react at lower levels than other people. During the 2023 Canadian wildfire smoke episodes, asthma emergency visits across the United States ran 17% higher than expected on smoke days. You can cut your risk by checking the Air Quality Index, staying indoors with filtered air on bad days, wearing a fitted N95 when you must go out, and following your asthma action plan.

How do PM2.5 and ozone affect the airways?

Wildfire smoke is a mix of gases and fine particles from burning trees, plants, buildings, and other material, according to the CDC. The particles that matter most are PM2.5, particles 2.5 micrometers across or smaller. The EPA notes that small particles can get deep into your lungs, and some may even get into your bloodstream. The EPA lists aggravated asthma, coughing, and difficulty breathing among the documented effects.

A 2025 narrative review in PMC describes the mechanism. PM2.5 drives overproduction of reactive oxygen species inside airway cells. That oxidative stress sets off a cascade of inflammatory signals in the airway lining. The review also reports that wildfire PM2.5 appears more harmful than urban PM2.5 of the same amount, likely because of differences in the chemical compounds bound to the particles.

Ozone is a different pollutant with a similar result. The EPA says ozone can cause the muscles in the airways to constrict, inflame and damage the airways, aggravate asthma, and increase the frequency of asthma attacks. The AAAAI calls ozone one of the most important pollutants and one of the most likely to cause asthma attacks. Both peak on hot summer days, one reason weather changes can worsen asthma.

What do the AQI colors mean if you have asthma?

The Air Quality Index runs from 0 to 500. AirNow divides it into six color bands:

  • Green, Good (0 to 50). Air pollution poses little or no risk.
  • Yellow, Moderate (51 to 100). Acceptable, but there may be a risk for people who are unusually sensitive to air pollution.
  • Orange, Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups (101 to 150). Members of sensitive groups may experience health effects. The general public is less likely to be affected.
  • Red, Unhealthy (151 to 200). Some members of the public may experience health effects. Sensitive groups may experience more serious effects.
  • Purple, Very Unhealthy (201 to 300). Health alert. The risk of health effects is increased for everyone.
  • Maroon, Hazardous (301 and above). Health warning of emergency conditions. Everyone is more likely to be affected.

People with asthma count as a sensitive group for both ozone and particle pollution. That means your threshold is orange, not red. The EPA guide for clinicians says sensitive groups may experience health effects during outdoor activities once the AQI passes 100. The same guide says people with asthma should carefully follow their asthma action plans when particle levels are high.

How much do asthma ER visits rise during smoke events?

The clearest national data come from the CDC MMWR report on the 2023 Canadian wildfire smoke. CDC analyzed syndromic surveillance data from about 4,317 emergency departments between April 30 and August 4, 2023. It defined a smoke day as one where PM2.5 pushed the AQI to 101 or higher. There were 19 such days. Asthma-associated ED visits were 17% higher than expected on those days. In the New York and New Jersey region alone, two June days produced 364 excess asthma visits.

A time-series study in Reno, Nevada covered 2013 to 2018. On days without smoke, a 5 microgram per cubic meter rise in PM2.5 barely changed asthma visits. On wildfire smoke days, that same rise increased asthma visits by 6.1% (95% CI 2.1% to 10.3%).

Does an N95 help, and do cloth masks work?

A fitted N95 helps. Cloth masks do not. EPA guidance names only particulate respirators tested by NIOSH, with the words NIOSH and either N95 or P100 printed on them. Worn correctly, EPA says these will significantly reduce your exposure to smoke particles. The respirator must have two straps that go around your head and must seal tightly to your face. Masks with one strap or with ear loops are not designed to seal, so they will not protect your lungs from smoke. Cloth masks, bandanas, and surgical masks fall in that group.

Two cautions apply to people with asthma. EPA says a respirator can make it harder to breathe, so anyone with heart or lung disease should ask a doctor before using one. And NIOSH-approved respirators do not come in sizes for young children, even though children 2 and older can wear one. A mask is a backup for time you must spend outside. Staying indoors with filtered air comes first.

How do you keep indoor air clean during smoke?

Start by keeping windows and doors closed and using air conditioning to stay cool, per the EPA. Then filter the air with one or both of these tools:

  1. Upgrade your HVAC filter. EPA recommends a MERV 13 filter, or the highest rating your system fan and filter slot can handle. The EPA indoor filtration factsheet says a MERV 13 to 16 filter can cut indoor particles by as much as 95 percent. If you cannot change filters, switching the thermostat fan from Auto to On has reduced particles by as much as 24 percent.
  2. Run a portable HEPA air cleaner. Pick one sized for the room, and confirm it does not produce ozone. Portable cleaners with high-efficiency filters can reduce indoor particles by as much as 85 percent. Ozone generators should never be used in occupied rooms.

Set up one clean room where you spend most of your time, with the air cleaner running and doors shut. Avoid anything that adds fine particles indoors: smoking, candles, incense, frying, gas or wood stoves, aerosol sprays, and vacuuming without a HEPA vacuum. A box fan with a MERV 13 filter is an accepted stopgap. EPA testing found these DIY cleaners reduce smoke, but only use a fan made in 2012 or later with a UL or ETL safety mark.

What should your asthma action plan say about smoke days?

An asthma action plan, as the NHLBI describes it, lists your triggers, which medicines to take and when, how to recognize an attack, and when to call your provider or go to the emergency room. Smoke and high-ozone days belong in the trigger section next to your other common asthma triggers. Ask your doctor to write down what changes when the AQI turns orange or red. Steps often include:

  • Check AirNow.gov each morning during fire season and before outdoor exercise.
  • Take your controller medicine every day as prescribed.
  • Keep your rescue inhaler with you at all times. The AAAAI advises using it as directed on bad pollution days.
  • Move workouts indoors when the AQI is 101 or higher. Skip outdoor exercise if you smell smoke or your eyes and throat sting.
  • Know your red-zone signs and follow your asthma attack action plan without delay.

Smoke can stay in the air for days after a fire ends, so keep these steps going until the AQI returns to green or yellow. Smoke symptoms such as cough, headache, and fast heartbeat overlap with other conditions. symptom.md can help you sort out what to report to your doctor. Get emergency care for severe shortness of breath, trouble speaking, or a rescue inhaler that does not help.

The bottom line

Air pollution and wildfire smoke are proven asthma triggers. PM2.5 and ozone inflame and tighten the airways, and wildfire particles look more harmful than the same amount of urban pollution. National data show asthma ER visits 17% above normal on smoke days. Treat an AQI above 100 as your signal to act. Stay inside with a MERV 13 filter or HEPA cleaner running, wear a fitted N95 if you must be outside, and follow the smoke-day steps in your action plan. Talk with your doctor before fire season so the plan is ready when the sky turns gray.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what AQI number should someone with asthma stay indoors?

Treat 101 as your line. People with asthma are a sensitive group, so the orange band (101 to 150) applies to you even though the general public is less likely to be affected. At red (151 to 200) and above, the risk rises for everyone. Check AirNow.gov for your ZIP code, since smoke levels can change within hours.

Is wildfire smoke worse for asthma than normal city pollution?

The evidence says yes at the same particle concentration. A Reno, Nevada study found a 5 microgram per cubic meter rise in PM2.5 increased asthma emergency visits by 6.1% on smoke days, with almost no effect on non-smoke days. Researchers think the chemicals bound to wildfire particles explain part of the difference.

Will a surgical mask or bandana protect my lungs from smoke?

No. EPA guidance recommends only NIOSH-approved N95 or P100 respirators with two head straps that seal to the face. Masks with ear loops or one strap, including surgical masks and cloth masks, are not designed to seal and will not filter out smoke particles. If you have asthma, ask your doctor before using a respirator, because it can make breathing feel harder.

Should I take extra asthma medicine on a smoky day?

Do not change doses on your own. Keep taking your daily controller medicine as prescribed and carry your rescue inhaler. Your asthma action plan should say what to do when symptoms start or your peak flow drops. Ask your doctor before fire season whether the plan needs a specific smoke-day section.

How long after a wildfire does the air stay dangerous?

The CDC says smoke can stay in the air for days after a wildfire ends, and smoke from distant fires can travel hundreds of miles. Keep following the AQI rather than judging by what you see or smell. Continue indoor filtration and outdoor limits until readings return to green or yellow.
