Can You Have Asthma If Your Spirometry Test Is Normal?

October 1, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes. Asthma is a variable disease, so your airways can be wide open on the day of the test and narrow a week later. A normal spirometry result between flares does not rule asthma out. Guidelines tell clinicians to keep testing with bronchodilator response, peak-flow diaries, FeNO, or a bronchial challenge when symptoms still point to asthma.

Why can spirometry be normal if you have asthma?

Asthma comes and goes. The GINA 2026 summary guide defines it by two features: respiratory symptoms that vary over time, and expiratory airflow that varies too. "Variable" is the key word. Symptoms and airway narrowing are worse at some times than at others.

Spirometry is a snapshot. It records how your lungs work during a few minutes in a clinic. If you feel well that morning, the airways may be fully open and the numbers may sit in the normal range. Many people with asthma feel worst at night or in the early morning, after exercise, or during a cold. Clinic visits rarely fall at those times. Our post on why asthma is worse at night explains the daily swing.

Treatment also hides the signal. GINA notes that people already taking an inhaled corticosteroid may need extra tests to confirm the diagnosis. A recent dose of a reliever inhaler can do the same. The European Respiratory Society (ERS) guideline also flags how hard a correct diagnosis is in patients already on inhaled corticosteroids.

What does spirometry actually measure?

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute describes spirometry as a test of how much air you breathe out and how fast. You take a full breath in, then blow out as hard and as long as you can. Two numbers matter most:

  • FEV1: the volume of air you force out in the first second.
  • FVC: the total volume you force out in one full breath.

Clinicians compare each number with the value predicted for your age, height, and sex. They also look at the FEV1/FVC ratio. A low ratio means air leaves the lungs slowly, which is called airflow obstruction. A normal ratio on a good day tells the clinician the airways were open at that moment. It says nothing about last night.

What counts as a positive bronchodilator test?

A bronchodilator responsiveness test (also called reversibility) repeats spirometry after a reliever medicine. GINA describes measuring spirometry before and 10 to 15 minutes after inhaled salbutamol (albuterol). A big jump shows that the airways were narrower than they should be. The cut-offs differ by guideline:

  • GINA 2026, adults: FEV1 or FVC rises by at least 12% and at least 200 mL from the pre-bronchodilator value.
  • ERS/ATS 2022 interpretive standard: the technical standard updated how bronchodilator response is judged. A study comparing the old and new criteria states the new rule as a change of more than 10% relative to the predicted value.
  • NICE, BTS and SIGN 2024, adults: the joint UK guideline accepts either rule. Asthma is diagnosed if FEV1 rises by 12% or more and 200 mL or more, or by 10% or more of the predicted normal FEV1.

The two rules use different yardsticks. One compares the change with your own starting number. The other compares it with the number predicted for a person like you. The same test can pass one rule and fail the other.

A negative result still does not close the question. If lung function is normal, GINA suggests repeating the test when you have symptoms, or after withholding bronchodilators if that is safe. GINA lists more than 4 hours for a short-acting reliever. GINA also notes that a bronchodilator response may be absent during a viral respiratory infection.

How do peak-flow diaries and FeNO help?

These two tests look for evidence that one clinic spirometry session can miss.

Peak-flow variability. A peak-flow diary measures your lungs at home, twice a day, over days. That catches the morning dips. GINA counts average daily variability above 10% in adults (above 13% in children) over 2 weeks as excessive. The UK guideline uses a different calculation and a different cut-off: twice-daily readings for 2 weeks, with variability of 20% or more. GINA adds that peak flow is less reliable than spirometry, but it is useful when spirometry is not available. See what a peak flow meter reading tells you for the technique.

FeNO. Fractional exhaled nitric oxide is a gentle breath test. NHLBI explains that high levels may mean the airways are inflamed. GINA says a FeNO above 50 ppb in adults and adolescents (above 35 ppb in children) can support the diagnosis in a person with typical symptoms. The UK guideline diagnoses asthma in adults at 50 ppb or more, or when the blood eosinophil count is above the laboratory reference range.

FeNO has limits. GINA states that a high result can come from conditions other than asthma, and that a low result does not rule asthma out. Levels vary with sex, age, device, and time of day.

The order of tests also differs. The 2022 ERS guideline on diagnosing asthma in adults starts with spirometry. If spirometry shows no obstruction, it recommends FeNO next, then peak-flow variability, then a bronchial challenge in secondary care. The UK guideline starts adults with a blood eosinophil count or FeNO and moves to spirometry second.

When is a methacholine challenge used?

A bronchial challenge is the usual next step when the other tests are negative and asthma is still suspected. The UK guideline says to refer for a challenge test at that point and to diagnose asthma if bronchial hyper-responsiveness is present.

The test checks how sensitive the airways are. NHLBI lists the agents used, which include inhaled methacholine, mannitol, histamine, and exercise. In a methacholine challenge you inhale rising doses and repeat spirometry after each one. The ERS technical standard bases the result on the delivered dose that causes a 20% fall in FEV1.

The test is done in a specialist lung function lab. GINA advises against bronchial challenge testing during pregnancy.

What conditions can mimic asthma?

Normal tests can also mean the symptoms have another cause. GINA reports that 25% to 35% of patients in primary care with an asthma diagnosis may not have asthma. Conditions that GINA names as look-alikes or co-existing problems include:

  • Inducible laryngeal obstruction (often called vocal cord dysfunction).
  • Upper airway cough syndrome (postnasal drip) and chronic sinusitis.
  • Gastroesophageal reflux. See can acid reflux make asthma worse.
  • ACE inhibitor cough. These are common blood pressure medicines, covered at hypertension.md.
  • Eosinophilic bronchitis.
  • COPD, mainly in people who smoked or were exposed to biomass fuel. A person can have asthma and COPD together.
  • Heart failure or ischemic heart disease, which can cause breathlessness in older adults.

One variant is easy to miss. In cough-variant asthma, cough may be the only symptom and the bronchodilator test may be negative. GINA says a bronchial challenge detects the airway hyper-responsiveness in these patients.

The bottom line

A normal spirometry result tells you the airways were open during the test. It does not prove you are free of asthma. If your symptoms come and go, wake you at night, or follow exercise or colds, tell your clinician and ask what test comes next. Keep a record of when symptoms happen. A peak-flow diary, FeNO, a repeat test during symptoms, or a bronchial challenge can settle the question. Tests can also show that the cause is something else, which matters just as much for getting the right treatment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a normal breathing test mean my symptoms are not asthma?

No. Asthma is defined by airflow that varies over time, so one normal spirometry session does not rule it out. GINA advises repeating the bronchodilator test when you have symptoms if lung function was normal. Further tests can then confirm or exclude asthma.

Should I stop my inhaler before a lung function test?

Ask the clinic that ordered the test, and do not stop a medicine on your own. GINA notes that a bronchodilator response is more likely to show if short-acting relievers are withheld for at least 4 hours, if that is safe. Longer-acting inhalers have longer withholding times.

What FeNO level points to asthma?

GINA says a FeNO above 50 ppb in adults and adolescents, or above 35 ppb in children, can support the diagnosis in a person with typical symptoms. The 2024 UK guideline uses 50 ppb or more in adults and 35 ppb or more in children aged 5 to 16. A lower level does not rule asthma out.

How much peak flow variation suggests asthma?

GINA counts average daily variability above 10% in adults, or above 13% in children, over 2 weeks as excessive. The 2024 NICE, BTS and SIGN guideline uses a different calculation with a cut-off of 20% or more. Your clinician will tell you which method they use.

How often is an asthma diagnosis wrong?

GINA reports that 25% to 35% of patients in primary care with an asthma diagnosis may not have asthma. This is why GINA asks clinicians to confirm the diagnosis with objective tests and to record the evidence. Do not stop treatment without talking to your clinician.
