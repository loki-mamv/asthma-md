Is It Safe to Take Ibuprofen or Aspirin If You Have Asthma?

The short answer: Most people with asthma can take ibuprofen or aspirin without a problem, but some cannot. About 7% of adults with asthma have aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD), and in them these drugs can set off an asthma attack within minutes to 2 hours. The over-the-counter labels for both drugs say to ask a doctor before use if you have asthma. If aspirin or an NSAID has ever made your breathing worse, avoid the whole group until you talk with a clinician.

How common is aspirin and NSAID sensitivity in asthma?

It affects a minority of adults with asthma. A 2015 meta-analysis in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology pooled 27 studies. Among all studies in asthmatic patients, the prevalence of AERD was 7.15%. In patients with severe asthma it was 14.89%.

The 2026 strategy report from the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) uses the same figures: 7% in general adult asthma populations and 15% in severe asthma. The American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology (AAAAI) gives a slightly higher estimate of about 9% of all adults with asthma.

That leaves most adults with asthma able to take these drugs. GINA states that aspirin and NSAIDs "are not generally contraindicated in asthma unless there is a history of previous reactions to these agents." It also tells clinicians to advise patients to stop an NSAID if asthma worsens.

What is aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease?

AERD is also called Samter's triad or NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease. The AAAAI describes three features that occur together:

  • Asthma
  • Sinus disease with nasal polyps that keep coming back
  • Respiratory reactions to aspirin and other NSAIDs that block an enzyme called cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1)

It is not a typical allergy. The AAAAI says the exact cause of the reactions is not known. No single blood test confirms AERD, so the diagnosis rests on the three features.

The reaction is to the whole drug class. An AAAAI work group report lists aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, indomethacin, and ketorolac among the highly selective COX-1 inhibitors. A person who reacts to one of them can react to the others.

Avoiding NSAIDs prevents the reactions, but it does not cure the condition. The AAAAI says patients still have asthma, nasal congestion, and recurrent polyps even with complete avoidance. Our guide to the connection between asthma and allergies covers how nose and sinus disease affect the lungs.

Who is at higher risk of a reaction?

The sources point to four groups:

  • People with nasal polyps. The AAAAI says about 30% of patients with both asthma and nasal polyps have AERD.
  • People with severe asthma. The meta-analysis found about twice the prevalence in severe asthma. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) says aspirin "may cause serious breathing problems in people with asthma that is difficult to treat."
  • Adults whose asthma started in adulthood. The AAAAI says AERD usually develops quite suddenly between the ages of 20 and 50.
  • People who react to alcohol. About 75% of patients with AERD get mild-to-moderate respiratory reactions when they drink alcohol, often after less than one glass.

The MedlinePlus page on ibuprofen reflects the first group. It says to tell your doctor if you have asthma, "especially if you also have frequent stuffed or runny nose or nasal polyps."

What does a reaction look like, and how fast does it start?

It starts fast. GINA says that after a person with AERD swallows aspirin or an NSAID, an acute asthma attack develops within minutes to 1 to 2 hours.

The reaction usually involves the nose and the chest together. The AAAAI and GINA list these signs:

  • Sudden nasal congestion, a runny nose, and sneezing
  • Sinus pain or a headache across the forehead
  • Cough, wheezing, and chest tightness
  • Eye irritation and a red flush of the head and neck
  • Sometimes a rash, abdominal pain, or vomiting

Some reactions are severe. GINA says a reaction "may sometimes progress to severe bronchospasm, shock, loss of consciousness, and respiratory arrest." Treat trouble breathing after a pain reliever as an emergency. Follow your asthma attack action plan and call 911 if your rescue inhaler does not help.

What do the over-the-counter labels warn?

Both Drug Facts labels name asthma twice: once as a symptom of a reaction and once as a reason to ask a doctor first.

The Advil (ibuprofen) label carries this allergy alert: "Ibuprofen may cause a severe allergic reaction, especially in people allergic to aspirin." The symptoms it lists include hives, facial swelling, "asthma (wheezing)," and shock. The label says not to use the product "if you have ever had an allergic reaction to any other pain reliever/fever reducer." It says to ask a doctor before use if you have asthma.

The Bayer Genuine Aspirin label has a matching alert. It says aspirin "may cause a severe allergic reaction," with the same list of hives, facial swelling, "asthma (wheezing)," and shock. It says not to use aspirin if you are allergic to it "or any other pain reliever/fever reducer." Its "Ask a doctor before use" list also ends with "you have asthma."

Neither label tells every person with asthma to avoid the drug. Both tell you to check first. The same ibuprofen list also names high blood pressure, heart disease, and kidney disease. Our sister site hypertension.md covers blood pressure.

Which pain relievers may a clinician suggest instead?

For people with AERD, the sources name two options. Both come with conditions.

Acetaminophen. The AAAAI says acetaminophen "is usually safely tolerated at low doses." The AAAAI work group report classes it as a weak COX-1 inhibitor, which is why the dose matters. The Tylenol Regular Strength label has no asthma warning. Its allergy alert covers severe skin reactions, and its main warning is liver damage above the labeled daily maximum.

COX-2 selective drugs. Celecoxib is a prescription NSAID that mainly blocks a different enzyme, COX-2. A 2014 meta-analysis of blinded, placebo-controlled trials looked at people with AERD who took a COX-2 inhibitor. It found no significant difference from placebo in respiratory symptoms, nasal symptoms, or a fall in lung function of 20% or more. The trials enrolled people with stable mild-to-moderate asthma.

The same analysis found a small risk with "selective NSAIDs," which favor COX-2 but are not full COX-2 inhibitors. They caused respiratory symptoms in about 1 in 13 patients with AERD.

GINA puts both options under supervision. It says a COX-2 inhibitor or acetaminophen "may be considered" when a pain reliever is needed, with observation "for at least 2 hours after administration." Do not test a new pain reliever on your own if you have reacted before.

What is aspirin desensitization?

It is a specialist procedure for people with confirmed AERD. The AAAAI says it is done "by administering gradually increasing doses of aspirin in a hospital or clinic that specializes in such treatment." The goal is long-term daily aspirin, which in some patients can slow polyp regrowth and reduce the need for corticosteroids.

GINA reports that desensitization followed by daily aspirin can improve upper respiratory symptoms and quality of life, and can reduce the need for sinus surgery. GINA adds two cautions. Few double-blind studies have examined asthma outcomes, and the treatment carries "a significantly increased risk of adverse effects such as gastritis and gastrointestinal bleeding."

Not everyone is a candidate. The AAAAI work group report lists pregnancy, uncontrolled asthma, a history of stomach ulcers, and bleeding disorders among the contraindications. Never try this at home. Injectable biologics are another route for severe asthma and nasal polyps. Our guide on whether asthma biologics work covers them.

The bottom line

Ibuprofen and aspirin are safe for most people with asthma, but about 7% of adults with asthma react to them, and the rate is higher with severe asthma or nasal polyps. A reaction starts within minutes to 2 hours and can be life-threatening. Both over-the-counter labels say to ask a doctor before use if you have asthma. If you have ever reacted, avoid all NSAIDs and ask about acetaminophen, a COX-2 selective drug, or a referral to an allergist.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is naproxen (Aleve) safer than ibuprofen for people with asthma?

No. An AAAAI work group report lists naproxen with aspirin and ibuprofen as a COX-1 inhibitor. People with aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease can react to every drug in that group. Ask a clinician before you switch from one NSAID to another.

I have taken ibuprofen for years with no problem. Can I still become sensitive?

Yes, it is possible. The AAAAI says AERD usually develops quite suddenly in adulthood, most often between the ages of 20 and 50. New nasal polyps, a lost sense of smell, or wheezing after a pain reliever are reasons to see a clinician.

Does low-dose aspirin for the heart count?

Aspirin at any strength is still aspirin, and the Bayer label says to ask a doctor before use if you have asthma. Do not stop prescribed aspirin on your own. Tell the prescriber about any breathing reaction so the two risks can be weighed together.

Can children with asthma take ibuprofen?

The prevalence figures in this article come from adults, and the AAAAI describes AERD as an adult-onset condition. The ibuprofen label still lists asthma under "Ask a doctor before use." Check with your child's clinician, and stop the drug if wheezing gets worse.

How do doctors confirm aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease?

A history of an asthma flare after aspirin or another NSAID is highly suggestive, according to GINA. When the history is unclear, a specialist can run a supervised aspirin challenge. GINA says oral challenges must only be done in a specialized center because of the high risk of severe reactions.
