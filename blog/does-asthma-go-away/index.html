Does Asthma Ever Go Away?

August 23, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: symptoms can stop, but "gone" is the wrong word. Doctors use remission, and it is genuinely common in childhood-onset asthma: reported rates run from about 15% to 64% by early adulthood, most often between ages 14 and 21. It is much rarer in adult-onset asthma, where one 15-year follow-up put remission at 11%. Remission is also not permanent. In the Dunedin birth cohort, 27.4% of people who had wheezed went into remission, and 12.4% relapsed by age 26. The underlying airway tendency often stays even when symptoms do not.

That distinction has one very practical consequence, covered at the end: what "my asthma went away" should mean for the inhaler in your drawer.

What does remission actually mean?

Clinically, remission means no asthma symptoms and no need for asthma medication over a defined period, usually at least 12 months. Some definitions add a normal lung function test, and stricter research definitions add the absence of airway hyperresponsiveness, meaning the airways no longer overreact on a challenge test.

That last item is where "remission" separates from "cure." Plenty of people stop having symptoms while their airways still respond abnormally to a provocation test. Symptomatically they are well. Physiologically the tendency is still there, waiting for the right trigger.

The wide range in reported remission rates, roughly 15% to 64%, is mostly explained by which definition a study used. Studies asking only "any symptoms in the past year?" find high remission. Studies that also require normal lung function and no airway hyperresponsiveness find much less.

How often does childhood asthma remit?

The best long-term data come from birth cohorts that followed the same people for decades.

The Dunedin study in New Zealand followed children born in 1972 and 1973 from age 9 to age 26 with questionnaires, lung function tests, bronchial challenge testing, and allergy testing (Sears et al., NEJM 2003;349:1414-1422). Among study members who had ever wheezed:

  • 14.5% had wheezing that persisted continuously from childhood to age 26.
  • 27.4% went into remission.
  • 12.4% relapsed by age 26.

A more recent population-based cohort followed participants from age 8 to age 28. Of the 170 people assessed at 28, 105 (61.8%) still had asthma, which means roughly 38% were in remission.

Two things to take from these numbers. Remission is common enough that hoping for it is reasonable. And relapse is common enough that treating remission as permanent is not.

Who is most likely to outgrow it?

The predictors are consistent across cohorts.

More likely to remit:

  • Mild disease in childhood, with little airway inflammation before adolescence.
  • Male sex.
  • Absence of allergic sensitization.
  • Normal lung function.

More likely to persist or relapse. The Dunedin study quantified these:

  • Sensitization to house dust mites: odds ratio 2.41 for persistence, 2.18 for relapse.
  • Airway hyperresponsiveness: odds ratio 3.00 for persistence, 3.03 for relapse. This was the strongest single predictor in the study.
  • Female sex: odds ratio 1.71 for persistence.
  • Smoking at age 21: odds ratio 1.84 for persistence.
  • Early age at onset, and more severe childhood disease. In people with severe childhood asthma, persistence into adulthood has been reported at approximately 90%.

One of those is a choice. Smoking is the only item on the persistence list you can change, and it nearly doubled the odds of persistent wheezing.

Allergic sensitization matters enough to be worth testing for, because it also changes management. Our guide to the asthma and allergy connection covers that overlap.

What about adult-onset asthma?

Different disease, different odds. Remission of adult-onset asthma is uncommon: a 15-year follow-up study reported a remission rate of 11%, and roughly one in six people with adult-onset asthma achieves clinical remission within the first five years.

Adult-onset asthma also tends to be less allergic in character, more often associated with obesity, occupational exposures, or nasal polyps, and generally more persistent. If your asthma started at 40, planning around remission is not a realistic strategy.

Why does asthma come back?

Because remission is usually a truce with the trigger environment rather than the end of the disease.

The common relapse triggers are recognizable: a respiratory infection, a new pet or a house move that changes allergen exposure, starting to smoke or living with a smoker, a new occupational exposure, pregnancy, and, in some people, a course of a medication that provokes bronchospasm. Non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol are contraindicated in asthma for exactly this reason, which our sister site anxiety.md touches on when discussing beta-blockers for anxiety.

Relapse can also be slow and easy to miss: a cough at night that you attribute to a lingering cold, breathlessness on hills you used to manage, a chest that tightens in cold air. Our guide to nighttime asthma covers that pattern.

What should you do if you are in remission?

Five things, and none of them require you to keep taking daily medication if your clinician agrees you no longer need it.

  1. Do not stop a controller inhaler on your own. Stepping down treatment is a supervised decision, made after a period of good control, and it is normally done gradually with review.
  2. Keep a rescue inhaler and check the expiry date. A reliever you cannot find during a chest infection is the most avoidable problem in this entire article.
  3. Tell every new clinician about the history. "Childhood asthma, in remission" changes prescribing decisions, particularly for beta-blockers and for some anti-inflammatory drugs.
  4. Do not start smoking, and do not vape. This is the single modifiable predictor of persistence in the cohort data.
  5. Know your own early warning signs. Night cough, exercise limitation, and needing a reliever more than twice a week are the standard signals that control is slipping. Our asthma action plan guide covers what to do at each stage.

The bottom line

Childhood asthma frequently goes quiet. Depending on the definition used, 15% to 64% of children are in remission by early adulthood, most commonly between ages 14 and 21. But in the Dunedin cohort, 12.4% relapsed by 26, and airway hyperresponsiveness roughly tripled the odds of both persistence and relapse.

Adult-onset asthma is a different picture: remission was 11% over 15 years in one follow-up study.

Treat remission as a good outcome you should protect, not as a cure you can forget about. Keep the diagnosis in your medical record, keep a reliever available, do not smoke, and step down medication with a clinician rather than on your own.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can asthma go away completely?

Symptoms can stop, which clinicians call remission, but that is not the same as a cure. Remission means no symptoms and no need for asthma medication over a defined period, usually at least 12 months. Stricter definitions also require normal lung function and no airway hyperresponsiveness. Many people who are symptom-free still have airways that overreact on a challenge test.

What percentage of children outgrow asthma?

Reported remission rates range from about 15% to 64% by early adulthood, and remission occurs most commonly between ages 14 and 21. The wide range reflects differing definitions of remission. In the Dunedin birth cohort followed from age 9 to 26, 27.4% of people who had wheezed went into remission and 14.5% had wheezing that persisted throughout.

Can asthma come back after it goes away?

Yes. In the Dunedin cohort, 12.4% of participants relapsed by age 26. The strongest predictors of both persistence and relapse were airway hyperresponsiveness (odds ratio 3.00 for persistence and 3.03 for relapse) and sensitization to house dust mites (odds ratio 2.41 and 2.18). Female sex and smoking at age 21 also predicted persistence.

Does adult-onset asthma go away?

Rarely. One 15-year follow-up study reported a remission rate of 11% in adult-onset asthma, and roughly one in six people achieves clinical remission within the first five years. Adult-onset asthma tends to be more persistent than childhood-onset asthma.

Should you stop your inhaler if your asthma symptoms have gone?

Not on your own. Stepping down asthma treatment is a supervised decision made after a sustained period of good control, and it is usually done gradually with review. Keep a rescue inhaler available and in date, tell every new clinician about the asthma history, and avoid smoking, which nearly doubled the odds of persistent wheezing in cohort data.
